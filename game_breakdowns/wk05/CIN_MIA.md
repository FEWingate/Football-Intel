## 1. PREGAME BRIEFING

Cincinnati enters at 2-2, carrying one of the more lopsided team identities in the league: a passing offense ranking 5th in yards per game (292.8) built around a historically pass-heavy approach (the Bengals' running back room ranks dead last in rush yards per game, 74.2, 31st), paired with a defense that is excellent against the run (99.5 rush yards allowed per game, 8th) and seldom sacked, but which allows a below-average 44.0% third-down conversion rate (23rd) and has been shredded by tight ends all year (102.2 receiving yards per game allowed to the position, 32nd — worst in the league).

Miami is 0-4 and owns the NFL's least productive offense by a wide margin: 11.5 points per game (32nd), 178.0 passing yards per game (31st), and a single passing touchdown through four games (32nd, tied for fewest in football). The Dolphins' running back room has been in flux — De'Von Achane, last year's workhorse, has seen his role shrink to a near-afterthought over the last two games while Ollie Gordon II has taken over as the lead back, a shift the team's own current depth chart now confirms. Miami's defense is a study in contrasts: elite against wide receivers (110.2 receiving yards allowed per game, 5th; fewest wide receiver targets faced in the league) but one of the worst units in football at containing running backs and tight ends in the passing game.

Broadly, this profiles as a game defined far more by what Miami's offense cannot do than by anything Cincinnati's defense needs to do exceptionally well — Cincinnati's passing attack, with its backfield receiving role as a real weapon, has a wide number of avenues against a defense that funnels production toward exactly those spots. Miami's path runs through an emerging ground game and whatever its own defense can manufacture against a Bengals offense that leans almost entirely on the pass. How contested this actually stays will come down to specific levers worked through below, not simply the gap in season-long numbers.

That game happened to also be one of his two road starts, so the mid-tier-specific and road-specific numbers converge on the same outing; this is a recurring pattern below since Pittsburgh (Week 3) was Cincinnati's only true mid-tier opponent this season and also fell on the road.

The larger structural point: Burrow will face one of the league's least threatening pass rushes. Miami generates just 1.0 sacks per game (30th, third-worst in football), while Burrow's own protection has been solid (2.2 sacks allowed/game, 19th). - **Nuclear** — player ranks top 3 in the category AND the opponent's defense ranks 30th or worse in that category
- **Elite** — player ranks top 5 AND opponent defense ranks 28th or worse
- **Standard** — player ranks top 10 AND opponent defense ranks 23rd or worse

A "Double," "Triple," or "Quadruple" designation reflects how many categories converged for that player.

## 6. HIDDEN INTELLIGENCE & CONTEXTUAL ANALYSIS

**Finding 1: Miami's pass defense is genuinely elite against wide receivers but is simultaneously one of the worst units in football against running backs in the passing game — a split masked by an unremarkable overall pass-yardage number.**

Miami's defense ranks a middling 19th in total passing yards allowed (235.0/game) — a number that, read in isolation, suggests an average pass defense. But that overall figure is hiding two sharply opposite realities. Against wide receivers specifically, Miami is excellent: 5th in receiving yards allowed (110.2/game), and it has faced the fewest wide receiver targets of any defense in the league (51 total, 1st) — both a function of strong coverage and, plausibly, opposing game plans already avoiding the matchup. Against running backs in the passing game, by contrast, Miami is one of the league's worst units: it has allowed 4 running back receiving touchdowns this season, tied for the most in football (32nd), and ranks a below-average 28th in running back receiving yards allowed (41.5/game). A reader scanning Miami's overall pass defense rank would reasonably assume a roughly league-average unit across the board; the actual picture is a defense that has specifically funneled production away from wide receivers and directly into backfield targets.

This connects directly to Cincinnati's own personnel: Chase Brown is already a heavily-used receiving back in neutral terms, with an elite 6.5 receptions per game (3rd among all running backs) on an 8.0-target-per-game workload (5th). Brown's own contextual splits (Context Expansion) make the case stronger still — his receiving production against bottom-tier defensive competition this season has nearly tripled his output against top-tier competition (45.3 receiving yards per game in his two bottom-tier games this season versus 18.0 in his single top-tier game, though both are thin samples). Miami's specific weakness at defending backs through the air is not a generic "bad pass defense" story; it is a precise match for the one role Cincinnati's offense already leans on heavily. Expect Brown's receiving role, not just his rushing workload, to be a focal point of Cincinnati's game plan — both as scheme-designed targets and as a checkdown outlet against Miami's elite-rushing pass rush (just 1.0 sacks/game, 30th) creating fewer coverage busts downfield. Confidence: MEDIUM — the team-level rank evidence (Miami's RB-receiving weakness) is strong and multi-category, but Brown's own tiered splits rest on very small samples (n=1 and n=3).

**Finding 2: Miami's backfield hierarchy has fully inverted over the last two games — and the player with the far stronger track record is now third on his own team's depth chart, with his per-touch efficiency collapsing in step with his shrinking role.**

Entering this season, De'Von Achane was unambiguously Miami's lead back, with a 2025 season-final track record of 84.38 rushing yards per game and 30.5 receiving yards per game on a 58.5% rush share and a 19.4% target share — a true three-down workhorse. That identity has visibly broken down in real time this season: his rush share fell to 9.7% in Week 3 (just 3 carries on 5% of offensive snaps) and he does not appear in the evidence package's game log at all for Week 4 — no carries, no targets, no snaps recorded. Over the same two-game stretch, Ollie Gordon II's role has grown in the opposite direction — a 54.8% rush share and 17 carries (with a touchdown) in Week 3, followed by a 50.0% rush share, 9 carries, and a 100-yard effort in Week 4. Miami's own current depth chart (dated October 7, 2026, directly ahead of this game) now lists Gordon at pos_rank 1 and even Jaylen Wright — a back averaging just 5.67 rushing yards per game this season — ahead of Achane at pos_rank 3.

This is not simply a case of declining touch share; Achane's underlying per-touch efficiency has fallen in exactly the same direction, which makes this read far more convincing than a role change driven by game script alone. His signature trait in 2025 was explosive, broken-tackle rushing — 3.02 yards after contact per carry and 0.88 broken tackles per game. This season those same numbers have collapsed to 1.38 yards after contact per carry and effectively no standalone broken-tackle production in his limited recent work. The condition (a real depth-chart demotion, confirmed independently of game logs) and the measurable shift (both touch share and per-touch efficiency moving the same direction at the same time) reinforce each other in a way that a single data point alone would not. No formal injury designation has been filed to explain why, which is itself worth flagging rather than assuming a specific cause. For this specific game: expect Ollie Gordon II to be Miami's clear lead back in carries and likely goal-line work, with Achane — if active at all — functioning in a sharply reduced, possibly change-of-pace-only role rather than anything resembling his 2025 workhorse usage. Confidence: MEDIUM — two straight games of consistent evidence plus independent depth-chart confirmation is a real signal, but the underlying cause (health, performance, or scheme) remains genuinely unresolved in this evidence package.

## 7. COEUS FINAL READ

**Keys to the Game**

If Cincinnati's pass rush (3.5 sacks/game, 3rd-best in the league) consistently gets to Malik Willis, his own career splits say the floor on Miami's offense drops even further — he is already markedly worse under pressure (45.2% completion, -0.422 EPA/play) than when kept clean, and clean pockets have not exactly been a strength for him either. This is the single most lopsided individual matchup in the game.

If Ollie Gordon II continues to handle the bulk of Miami's backfield work, the Dolphins have at least a credible path to sustaining drives on early downs — their own run defense-adjacent production against top-tier run defenses this season (70.5 yards per game across two feature outings) suggests Gordon can produce against stout fronts, even if Cincinnati's run defense (8th-9th ranked, 99.5 yards allowed per game) is a tougher assignment than either opponent he has faced in that role so far.

If Cincinnati leans into Chase Brown's receiving role and its tight end rotation (whichever of Gesicki or Sample sees the bulk of red-zone and intermediate work) rather than relying purely on Burrow's two outside receivers, it is attacking Miami's defense at its two genuinely soft points (running back and tight end receiving) rather than its one elite point (wide receiver coverage) — a materially higher-efficiency approach than simply feeding Higgins and Chase against a defense built to stop exactly that.

**The Verdict**

The broad shape this game was expected to take — a competitive but lopsided matchup driven more by Miami's offensive limitations than by any specific Cincinnati defensive dominance — holds up under the deeper look, with the specific levers now identified. Cincinnati's passing operation is matched against one of the league's least threatening pass rushes and a defense that, while elite against wide receivers, is specifically generous to the exact two spots (backfield and tight end targets) Cincinnati's offense is already built to exploit. Miami's one real counter-punch is a backfield that has quietly reorganized around a more productive lead back in Ollie Gordon II, set against a Cincinnati run defense that is stout in aggregate but has shown real explosive-play vulnerability underneath its ranking. Expect Cincinnati to control this game through its passing attack and backfield receiving usage, with Miami's competitiveness resting almost entirely on Gordon's rushing production and whatever its defense can manufacture turnovers or stops from — not on anything its own passing game, the worst in football through four weeks, is currently equipped to provide.
