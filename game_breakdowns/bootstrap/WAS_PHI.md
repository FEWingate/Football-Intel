## 1. GAME OVERVIEW

This is a rebuilding, 4-12 Washington team on the road against an 11-5 Philadelphia team that finished as a top-5 scoring defense in 2025. The season-long numbers paint a lopsided picture on paper, but the underlying identity of each team matters more than the win-loss column.

Washington's offense was bottom-third in nearly every meaningful category last season — 25th in points per game (20.8), 25th in passing yards per game (199.9), 25th in first downs per game (16.4) — with one real exception: the run game finished 4th in the NFL at 134.2 rush yards per game, a figure inflated by dual-threat quarterback production and a committee backfield rather than one workhorse. Washington's defense was worse across the board, finishing 27th in points allowed (27.1/g), 29th against the pass (258.1 yds/g), and dead last in the NFL against the run (145.4 yds/g, ranked 31st of 32).

Philadelphia's offense is more modest than the record suggests — 19th in scoring (22.6 ppg), 23rd passing (203.6 yds/g), 26th in first downs per game (16.4) — but the defense is legitimately elite in specific areas: 3rd in points allowed (18.8/g), 8th against the pass (209.1 yds/g), and 1st in the NFL in fewest passing touchdowns allowed (13 all season) and opponent completion percentage (56.6%). The run defense is more ordinary, 21st in the league at 123.2 yards allowed per game.

The shape of this game is straightforward given the evidence: Philadelphia's offense should be able to lean on the ground game against one of the league's worst run defenses, while Washington's offense — already inefficient through the air — faces a defense that doesn't allow big passing days but suffocates scoring chances once the ball crosses midfield. **Confidence: MEDIUM** on the overall shape — the specific game script depends heavily on early-down success, and Washington's offense has shown some spike weeks against quality competition despite the underlying numbers.

## 2. TEAM UNIT BREAKDOWN

### Washington Offense
Washington's passing attack was one of the least productive in football: 199.9 yards per game (25th), an anemic 27.9 attempts per game (31st) and 17.1 completions per game (31st) — evidence of a low-volume, low-college-passing environment rather than an inefficient-but-busy one. The one bright spot was yards per completion at 11.7 (7th), suggesting an offense built around chunk plays over West Coast rhythm passing. The running game was the actual strength — 134.2 yards per game (4th) — but the underlying team-context splits show remarkable stability across opponent tiers: 87.2 rush yards/game vs. top defenses (n=4), 92.2 vs. mid-tier (n=8), and 96.2 vs. bottom-tier (n=4). That flatness suggests Washington's ground production is more scheme/personnel-driven than opponent-exploitation-driven — it doesn't spike dramatically even against bad run defenses, which is a mild caution against assuming an automatic explosion versus Philadelphia's mid-tier (21st) run defense. **Confidence: MEDIUM.**

### Philadelphia Offense
Philadelphia's offense is unspectacular in raw team totals — 22.6 points/game (19th), 203.6 pass yards/game (23rd) — but the underlying context splits show something opponent-specific worth flagging: Philadelphia's rushing production scales hard against bad run defenses, averaging 105.8 rush yards/game vs. top-tier run defenses (n=4), 113.4 vs. mid-tier (n=7), and 145.0 vs. bottom-tier defenses (n=5). Washington's run defense (145.4 yards/game allowed, 31st) sits squarely in that bottom tier. Philadelphia's passing offense, by contrast, actually performed *worse* against mid-tier pass defenses (220.1 yds/g, n=7) than against top-tier ones (192.0 yds/g, n=5) — a modest inversion worth noting but not overweighting given the samples involved. **Confidence: MEDIUM-HIGH** on the run-game lean; **LOW** on reading anything conclusive from the passing-tier inversion given sample size.

### Washington Defense
This is the unit that defines the game. Washington ranked 31st in the NFL against the run (145.4 yds/g allowed) and 29th against the pass (258.1 yds/g allowed), 32nd in total yards allowed (403.5/g) and 32nd in first downs allowed per game (20.5). Positionally, the picture is uniformly bad: 29th in QB passing yards allowed, 31st in QB passing touchdowns allowed (32 total, worst in the league), 30th in RB rushing yards allowed, 27th in WR receiving yards allowed, and 26th in TE receiving yards allowed. There is no defensible strength here — this is a bottom-5 defense across all four core evaluation categories. **Confidence: HIGH.**

### Philadelphia Defense
The inverse story. Philadelphia ranked 3rd in points allowed (18.8/g) and 13th in total yards allowed (332.3/g), but the real separator is discipline in the passing game: 1st in the NFL in completion percentage allowed (56.6%) and 1st in passing touchdowns allowed (13 all season). That doesn't mean Philadelphia doesn't yield yardage — 8th in pass yards allowed per game (209.1) is good but not dominant — it means Philadelphia bends for yardage but does not break for touchdowns through the air. The tight end defense is the standout: Philadelphia ranked 1st in the NFL in TE receiving yards allowed per game (30.7), 1st in yards per reception allowed to tight ends (7.8), and 2nd in TE touchdowns allowed (3 all season). The run defense, by contrast, is the unit's soft spot: 21st in the league at 123.2 rush yards/g allowed. **Confidence: HIGH.**

### The Interaction
Washington's lone offensive strength (the run game) meets Philadelphia's lone defensive soft spot (21st against the run) — but Philadelphia's own offense stands to exploit that same Washington weakness far more severely, since Washington's run defense (31st) is considerably worse than Philadelphia's (21st). Washington's passing attack, already the weaker half of its offense, runs into a defense that specifically shuts down explosive/scoring pass plays. The matchup tilts toward Philadelphia controlling the game on the ground while limiting Washington's ability to counter through the air.

## 3. POSITIONAL MATCHUPS

### Quarterbacks

**Jalen Hurts (PHI)** is the clear, entrenched starter — 97.6% snap share across 16 games, 3,224 passing yards (201.5 yds/game, ranked 26th), 25 touchdowns, and a career-best 0.4 interceptions/game (2nd-fewest in the league). Hurts' season splits show he was more efficient facing zone-heavy opponents (64.1% completion, 44.8% success rate) than man-heavy ones (53.8% completion, 40.4% success), though his epa-per-play was actually higher against man coverage (0.078 vs. 0.039) — he throws fewer completions against man but they carry more value when they land. Washington's PHI-facing threat data shows Hurts projected against a defense allowing 258.1 pass yards/game (ranked 29th) and 2.0 passing touchdowns/game (ranked 31st, second-worst in the league) — a clearly generous matchup on paper. Blitz data shows Hurts is actually slightly *more* efficient without pressure (43.4% success vs. no blitz vs. 42.8% vs. blitz), and Washington projects to blitz him at a middling 26.5% rate (ranked 19th). **Confidence: HIGH** that the matchup favors Hurts statistically; **MEDIUM** on translating that into a monster individual box score given Philadelphia's run-first game-script tendency (see Hidden Intelligence).

**Washington's QB situation is unsettled in the underlying data.** Football Intel's Threat Intelligence lists **Jayden Daniels** as the presumptive starter (75.0% snap share referenced), but Daniels' own 2025 sample is limited to 7 games (180.3 pass yards/game, 90.3% snap share when active) — his absence for a large chunk of the season is evident in the data. **Marcus Mariota** started or relieved in 10 games (200.2 yards/game average, 74.5% snap share) and carries the larger, more stable 2025 sample; Football Intel's matchup-pattern data (blitz and rush/pass correlation) is built entirely on Mariota's game logs, not Daniels'. **Josh Johnson** also factored in for 3 late-season games as a third option. This report treats Daniels as the Week 1 starter per the Threat Intelligence designation but flags this as a genuine uncertainty the reader should track before kickoff. **Confidence: LOW** on which quarterback actually starts; the passing-game evaluation below leans on whichever pattern data is available (mostly Mariota's, given sample depth).

Whichever quarterback plays, the passing offense faces a Philadelphia defense allowing 209.1 pass yards/game (ranked 8th) and, critically, the fewest passing touchdowns in the league (13 total, ranked 1st) — a hard ceiling on upside through the air regardless of who's throwing. Mariota's own splits show a pronounced man/zone gap: 66.4% completion and a 55.7% success rate against zone looks vs. just 47.9% completion and 37.5% success against man — worth monitoring if Philadelphia mixes in more press-man than its base rate suggests.

### Running Backs

**Saquon Barkley (PHI)** is a clear workhorse — 61.1% of Philadelphia's rush share, 78.3% snap share across 16 games, 1,140 rushing yards (71.25 yds/game, ranked 11th) on 17.5 carries/game, plus 50.0% of the red-zone carry share and 40.8% of red-zone touches on the team. His season-long production shows real range: 58.2 rush yards/game against top-tier run defenses (n=6), 84.7 against bottom-tier (n=4) — and specifically against Washington's caliber of run defense (31st, 118.1 yards/game allowed to opposing RBs and 30th, 145.4 yards/game allowed as a team stat), Barkley's opportunity profile is about as favorable as this evidence package can project. His signature-analytics profile shows an inverse relationship with Hurts' passing volume (see Hidden Intelligence) — his rushing hit rate climbs from 16.7% in Hurts-hot passing games to 55.6% when Hurts is passing less, suggesting Barkley's role expands specifically when Philadelphia controls tempo. **Confidence: HIGH** on workload; **MEDIUM** on translating pure opportunity into an outlier statistical day given the sample-size caveats in the correlation data.

**Washington's backfield is a genuine committee** between **Jacory Croskey-Merritt** and **Chris Rodriguez Jr.**, and the two carry different roles worth separating for extraction purposes. Croskey-Merritt: 36.2% rush share, 39.5% snap share, 805 rushing yards (47.35 yds/game) on 10.29 carries/game, plus far more receiving work (13 targets on the season vs. Rodriguez's 4) and a 27.3% red-zone touch share. Rodriguez Jr.: 32.1% rush share, 31.2% snap share, 500 rushing yards (41.67 yds/game) on 9.33 carries/game, but a *higher* red-zone carry share at 41.8% (vs. Croskey-Merritt's 34.0%) and 6 rushing touchdowns to Croskey-Merritt's 8 on far fewer red-zone touches — evidence Rodriguez is deployed as more of a short-yardage/goal-line specialist while Croskey-Merritt handles early-down and passing-down work. Neither back projects for a positive game script against a top-8 Philadelphia scoring defense. **Confidence: MEDIUM** on role split; **LOW** on either back's ceiling given the likely game-script disadvantage.

### Wide Receivers / Tight Ends

**A.J. Brown (PHI)** leads Philadelphia's receiving corps with a 29.5% target share (highest on the team), 66.87 receiving yards/game over 15 games and 7 touchdowns. His man/zone splits favor explosive plays against man coverage specifically — 14.73 yards per play and a 0.37 epa-per-play mark against man vs. 11.49 yards/play and 0.209 epa against zone — and his blitz splits are even more pronounced: a 71.4% hit rate on his yardage threshold in high-blitz games versus just 37.5% in low-blitz games. Washington projects to blitz at a middling 26.5% rate (ranked 19th), so any uptick in pressure specifically raises Brown's ceiling. Facing Washington's WR defense (27th, 163.8 receiving yards/game allowed; 28th in yards per reception allowed), the matchup favors Brown on paper. **Confidence: MEDIUM-HIGH.**

**DeVonta Smith (PHI)** is the clear second option, 24.4% target share, 59.29 yards/game across all 17 games (the only Philadelphia skill player to play every game), with 4 touchdowns. His per-tier splits show real weakness against bottom-tier defenses specifically (48.7 yards/game, n=9) relative to top-tier defenses (104.0 yards/game, n=2) — a small-sample inversion worth flagging but not leaning on heavily given Washington's bottom-tier classification. **Confidence: LOW-MEDIUM** on translating that split into a specific projection.

**Dallas Goedert (PHI)** is a standalone Hidden Intelligence storyline in his own right — see below — but as a standard positional note: 20.0% target share, 39.4 receiving yards/game over 15 games, and an extraordinary red-zone role (26.8% red-zone target share, 15 red-zone targets, 13 red-zone receptions, and 10 of his 11 season touchdowns coming on red-zone targets). **Terry McLaurin (WAS)** carries a 23.3% target share and 58.2 receiving yards/game across 10 games (he missed time during the season), with a healthy 21.6% red-zone target share. **Deebo Samuel Sr. (WAS)** actually out-targets McLaurin on a rate basis at 24.7% target share and played all 16 games (45.44 yards/game), with a nearly identical red-zone target share (21.7%). Samuel's man-coverage splits are the standout number in this evidence package: 12.8 yards per play and an extreme 0.744 epa-per-play against man coverage (vs. 8.66 yards/play and -0.157 epa against zone) — Samuel is a legitimate man-beater when defenses press him, though he doesn't project a heavy target share advantage over McLaurin. Both face a Philadelphia WR defense that is middling in yardage allowed (15th, 138.3 yards/game) but elite at preventing touchdowns (2nd, only 6 WR touchdowns allowed all season) — a real dampener on upside for either Washington receiver. **Confidence: MEDIUM** for both.

**Zach Ertz (WAS)** carries a 20.2% target share and 38.77 yards/game over 13 games, with a significant red-zone role (16.4% red-zone target share, 4 red-zone touchdowns). His zone-beating profile is extreme — 11.76 yards/play and 0.505 epa against zone vs. just 6.82 yards/play and -0.393 epa against man — but he runs directly into Philadelphia's best individual unit: a defense that ranked 1st in the NFL against tight ends in yards allowed, yards-per-reception allowed, and 2nd in touchdowns allowed. **Confidence: MEDIUM-HIGH** that Ertz's per-target efficiency is meaningfully suppressed regardless of coverage shell Philadelphia shows.

**Depth notes for extraction:** Washington's Jaylin Lane (8.2% target share) and Luke McCaffrey (6.2% target share, 3 touchdowns on very low volume) offer situational upside; Treylon Burks carries a **Questionable** injury designation (see Section 7). ## 4. COVERAGE & SCHEME NOTES

Washington projects to blitz Jalen Hurts at a middling 26.5% rate (ranked 19th of 32), with roughly average success on those blitzes (53.8% success rate, ranked 20th) — nothing exotic, but enough pressure that Hurts' known efficiency dip under blitz (42.8% success vs. blitz, 43.4% vs. no blitz) could matter marginally. Philadelphia, on the other side, blitzes at a league-low 20.0% rate (ranked 29th) but is highly successful when it does bring extra rushers (62.5% success rate, ranked 4th in the league) — a "rare but effective" profile. That specifically matters against Mariota (or whichever Washington QB starts), whose splits actually show *better* efficiency against the blitz (52.8% success, 0.102 epa/play) than against a standard rush (43.9% success, -0.07 epa/play) — a counter-intuitive but real signal that Washington's passing game may not be as vulnerable to Philadelphia's occasional pressure as the low blitz-rate suggests.

In man-vs-zone terms, both team's top receiving weapons (A.J. Brown and Deebo Samuel) show clear man-coverage-explosive profiles, while both top tight ends (Dallas Goedert and Zach Ertz) are pronounced zone-beaters. This suggests the passing-down chess match for both offensive coordinators is straightforward: attack man coverage on the perimeter, attack zone through the seam/middle with the tight end.

Individually, Philadelphia's **Quinyon Mitchell** is the best cornerback on either roster by a wide margin — 45.1% completion percentage allowed and 5.43 yards allowed per target, both elite marks league-wide. If Mitchell shadows McLaurin or Samuel, that assignment materially caps Washington's top perimeter option. Washington's depth chart has no equivalent shutdown piece; **Marshon Lattimore** (59.5% completion allowed, 8.3 yards/target) and **Trey Amos** (71.1% completion allowed) are more replacement-level, and **Mike Sainristil** has allowed 9 touchdowns on the season, the highest mark among all sampled defensive backs in this evidence package. Washington safety **Quan Martin** stands out as the single weakest coverage piece in this game on either side — a 153.3 opponent passer rating allowed and 14.73 yards allowed per target, both far worse than any other player in the cb_db_rankings sample — a clear exploitation point if Philadelphia can identify and target him in coverage, particularly with Goedert working the middle of the field. **Confidence: MEDIUM-HIGH** on the Mitchell/Martin individual notes; **LOW** on projecting exact coverage assignments since specific defensive alignment plans aren't in evidence.

## 5. HIDDEN INTELLIGENCE

**Finding 1 — Barkley's workload and Hurts' passing volume move in opposite directions, but Washington's defense is bad enough on both sides that the pattern may not hold this week.** Saquon Barkley's rushing-yardage hit rate falls to just 16.7% (n=6) in games where Hurts' passing is above his own threshold, but climbs to 55.6% (n=9) when Hurts' passing is below it — a clear signal that Philadelphia leans on Barkley specifically when the passing game isn't clicking, rather than the two production streams running in parallel. This isn't obvious from season averages alone; it only appears when the two players' game logs are cross-referenced week-by-week. The complication for this specific game: Washington's defense is bottom-5 against *both* the run (31st) and the pass (29th), meaning Philadelphia may not need the passing game to be "cold" to justify heavy Barkley usage — both avenues are open simultaneously. **Confidence: MEDIUM** — the historical pattern is real, but this matchup may break the typical if-then relationship rather than confirm it.

**Finding 2 — Dallas Goedert is almost exclusively a red-zone weapon, and Washington's defense is specifically vulnerable there.** Of Goedert's 11 receiving touchdowns this season, 10 came on red-zone targets (13 red-zone receptions on 15 red-zone targets) — meaning essentially his entire touchdown production is concentrated in a part of the field a reader wouldn't isolate just from his 39.4 yards/game season average. Washington ranks 26th in the league in TE receiving yards allowed per game (61.8) with 11 total TE touchdowns allowed (31st). If Philadelphia's Barkley-driven run game (Finding 1) produces the expected volume of scoring opportunities against Washington's 31st-ranked run defense, Goedert's red-zone role becomes disproportionately valuable relative to his modest overall target share (20.0%). **Confidence: MEDIUM-HIGH.**

**Finding 3 — Zach Ertz's biggest strength (beating zone coverage) runs directly into the one defense built to neutralize it.** Ertz's per-play value against zone coverage is elite (11.76 yards/play, 0.505 epa/play) compared to man (6.82 yards/play, -0.393 epa/play) — ordinarily a green light for a plus matchup. But Philadelphia's tight end defense isn't just generically solid; it's the No. 1 unit in the NFL in TE yards allowed per game (30.7) and yards per reception allowed (7.8), regardless of coverage shell. A player's typical coverage-specific advantage doesn't automatically transfer against a defense whose overall scheme discipline against the position is this extreme — the two data points (Ertz's zone splits and Philadelphia's overall TE defense) point in opposite directions, and the overall defensive ranking should carry more weight given how far it separates from league average. **Confidence: MEDIUM.**

**Finding 4 — Washington's single weakest coverage defender is a safety, not a cornerback, which changes who the exploit target should be.** Across the cb_db_rankings sample, Quan Martin's 153.3 allowed passer rating and 14.73 yards allowed per target are outliers — the worst marks of any defensive back on either roster by a wide margin, including 6 touchdowns allowed. Safeties are typically the "clean-up" piece in a defense rather than the primary coverage matchup, so this isn't the type of weakness a reader would look for first (they'd check the cornerbacks). Given Goedert and Dotson's likely deployment through the middle of the field, and Philadelphia's general man-coverage passing advantages for its perimeter receivers, Martin represents a more identifiable individual soft spot than any Washington cornerback. **Confidence: MEDIUM** — sample size on Martin (49 targets) is reasonable, but we don't have confirmation he'll be schemed into these specific matchups.

## 8. KEYS TO THE GAME

1. **If Philadelphia establishes Saquon Barkley early against Washington's 31st-ranked run defense**, expect the run-funnel, ball-control game script that historically depresses Jalen Hurts' passing volume (Hidden Intelligence, Finding 1) — and expect Washington's offense, already 25th in points per game, to face a shortened number of possessions as a result.

2. **If Washington is forced into obvious passing situations** (trailing on the scoreboard), watch for a "yards without touchdowns" outcome — Philadelphia's defense yields modest yardage (8th in pass yards allowed) but is historically elite at preventing scores (1st in passing touchdowns allowed, 1st in completion percentage allowed). Washington moving the ball between the 20s without finishing drives is a real, evidence-supported possibility.

3. **If Philadelphia's possessions reach the red zone at an above-average rate** (likely, given the run-game mismatch), watch Dallas Goedert specifically — 10 of his 11 season touchdowns came on red-zone targets, and Washington's 26th-ranked TE defense doesn't project to slow him down there even if it holds up more respectably between the 20s.

4. **If Washington's pass rush generates any uptick in pressure** on early downs, A.J. Brown's blitz splits (71.4% yardage-threshold hit rate in high-blitz games vs. 37.5% in low-blitz games) suggest he's live for an explosive outing regardless of Washington's modest 26.5% season blitz rate — one or two well-timed blitz calls could be enough to trigger this.

## 9. BOTTOM LINE

This is a game where the evidence points in one direction almost uniformly: Philadelphia should be able to establish Saquon Barkley against the league's worst run defense, control the clock, and let an already-inefficient Washington passing attack run into a Philadelphia defense that specializes in preventing exactly the kind of explosive, scoring plays Washington would need to keep pace. Dallas Goedert's near-total red-zone touchdown concentration and Washington safety Quan Martin's status as the weakest coverage piece on either roster are the two most actionable, non-obvious threads in this evidence — both point toward Philadelphia's scoring efficiency being the story of the game rather than any explosive Washington counterpunch. **Overall confidence in this directional read: MEDIUM-HIGH**, tempered mainly by the genuine uncertainty over which quarterback starts for Washington and the absence of any Threat Intelligence convergence to independently corroborate the individual matchup edges identified here.
