## 4. MATCHUP INTELLIGENCE

### The Central Tension: San Francisco's Situational Dominance vs. Denver's Turnover Identity

The single most lopsided number in this evidence package is the third-down gap: San Francisco's offense converts third downs at a league-best 63.0% rate (1st) against a Denver defense that allows conversions at a bottom-quarter 47.8% rate (27th). That gap, on its own, argues San Francisco should be able to sustain long, clock-eating drives against this specific defense. The countervailing factor is Denver's defensive takeaway rate — 1st in the league in interceptions forced (1.3/gm) — which means Denver doesn't need to stop San Francisco on schedule to flip a drive; it needs one well-timed mistake. How those two forces resolve is close to the whole game.

### Quarterbacks

**Brock Purdy (SF).** Through three starts, Purdy is averaging 263.0 pass yards per game (9th in the league — *2025: 240.78 ypg*, so an early-season improvement, though the sample is still thin), with an elite 72.3% completion rate (2nd), 9 touchdowns against just 1 interception (0.33/gm, 12th), and — remarkably — zero sacks allowed through three games (1st in the league). His home/road and tier splits sharpen the picture further: he's at 292.0 pass yds/gm in his two home starts this season, and in the one game he's faced a bottom-tier pass defense (Arizona, Week 3 — the same tier Denver's defense occupies this week, 24th in the league), he threw for 297 yards and 4 touchdowns at a 150.9-plus-equivalent rating. That's a single-game sample (n=1), but it's the most relevant precedent available, and it lines up with Denver ranking 24th against the pass.

Purdy's blitz splits are the most counterintuitive piece of evidence in this report. Across 23 blitzed dropbacks this season he's completing 78.3% of his throws for +1.378 EPA/play and a 73.9% success rate — all better than his numbers when NOT blitzed (70.0% completion, +0.323 EPA/play, 58.3% success rate across 60 dropbacks). Most quarterbacks are more efficient with a clean, unpressured pocket; Purdy's early-season profile inverts that. Confidence: MEDIUM (strong, consistent splits, but still a 3-game sample).

**Bo Nix (DEN).** Averaging 201.7 pass yards per game (26th — *2025: 231.24 ypg*, a real decline worth flagging, though three games is early), with a 60.2% completion rate (24th), 4 touchdowns (20th), and 1.0 interceptions per game (23rd). Takes 1.7 sacks per game (9th-fewest, a positive), but the production has been starkly tiered: 158.5 pass yds/gm across his two tougher matchups (KC, LAR; n=2) against a 288.0-yard outlier in his one mid-tier matchup (JAX; n=1). San Francisco's pass defense (11th, mid-tier) sits in the same band as that one favorable data point — but it's a single game, so treat any optimism here as low-confidence. His own splits show a QB who struggles significantly against the blitz: 38.7% completion, 4.16 yards/play, and a damaging -0.402 EPA/play against the blitz (31 snaps) versus 63.8% completion and +0.079 EPA/play when not blitzed (69 snaps) — essentially the opposite profile of Purdy's. Confidence: MEDIUM.

Road/home-road note: Nix has played only one true road game this season (Week 1 at KC, his lone game on this split), so his road-specific numbers (131 pass yards, 60.7% completion, 69.2 rating) are the only road data available, and no tier-matched road split against a mid-tier pass defense exists yet (Denver hasn't faced one on the road this season) — a genuine evidence gap worth noting rather than guessing past.

## 5. THREAT INTELLIGENCE

Football Intel's deterministic Threat Engine flags a player when their own SEASON rank in a specific statistical category AND the upcoming opponent's defensive rank in that SAME category both clear a tier's threshold — the convergence of real individual production and a genuinely weak specific matchup, not either signal in isolation. The three tiers use fixed thresholds: **Nuclear** requires the player ranking top 3 in a category AND the opponent's defense ranking 30th or worse in that category; **Elite** requires top 5 and opponent 28th or worse; **Standard** requires top 10 and opponent 23rd or worse. A "Double" designation means one category met both thresholds; "Triple" adds a second category where the player's own rank alone clears the bar even if the opponent side doesn't fully converge.

**Denver side: no Threat designations fired for any starter** (Bo Nix, RJ Harvey, Courtland Sutton, Jaylen Waddle, Evan Engram). The reason is straightforward from the evidence above — none of Denver's offensive players currently rank inside the top 10 nationally in any of their primary categories (Nix's pass yards rank 23rd by the threat engine's own category computation; Sutton's rec yds rank 68th; Waddle's 31st; Engram's 28th), so no convergence was possible regardless of San Francisco's defensive ranks.

- **Brock Purdy — Standard tier, Triple type, converged on pass_yds.** Purdy's own pass-yards rank (8th) clears the Standard top-10 threshold, and Denver's pass defense rank in that category (24th) clears the Standard "23rd or worse" threshold — a genuine convergence. Pass TDs is the extra category: Purdy's own rank there (1st in the league, 3.0/gm) clears the top-10 bar on its own, even though Denver's defensive rank in that specific category (10th) doesn't independently qualify. Read against the rest of this report: this aligns cleanly with everything else said about Purdy above — his own excellent form, a real statistical opponent weakness, and a specific historical precedent (his 297-yard, 4-touchdown game against a similarly bottom-tier pass defense) all point the same direction. ## 6. HIDDEN INTELLIGENCE & CONTEXTUAL ANALYSIS

**Finding 1: Denver's red-zone identity clashes with exactly what San Francisco's defense is built to dare teams into.** Denver's offense is pass-heavy once it reaches the red zone — a 62.5% pass rate there, 6th-highest in the league, against just a 37.5% run rate (27th). San Francisco's defense, meanwhile, is specifically more willing to let teams run in the red zone (56.4% run rate allowed, 8th-highest) while tightening hard against the pass down there (43.6% pass rate allowed, 25th-lowest) — a defense that effectively bets teams won't finish drives on the ground. Denver's own tendency runs directly against that bet. Context Expansion: Denver's own contextual splits show its scoring output has already swung wildly with competition level — 15.0 points per game across its two tougher matchups (KC, LAR; n=2) against a 30-point outlier in its lone mid-tier matchup (JAX; n=1). San Francisco's defense carries only mid-pack yardage ranks (11th against the pass, 15th against the run) but ranks top-5 in points allowed (16.7/gm, 5th) — meaning by the metric that actually decides the scoreboard, San Francisco plays like one of Denver's "tough" matchups, not its "mid-tier" one. Combined with the red-zone scheme mismatch, Denver's realistic scoring expectation should trend closer to its 15.0-point range than its one 30-point outlier. Confidence: MEDIUM.

**Finding 2: Denver's league-best takeaway rate may be pointed at the one quarterback least likely to be rattled by it.** Denver's defense leads the NFL in interceptions forced (1.3/gm) and ranks top-10 in sacks (2.7/gm, 7th) and completion percentage allowed (55.9%, 2nd) — a profile that typically reflects aggressive pressure creating negative plays. Brock Purdy's own season-long splits run directly against the grain of what usually makes a quarterback vulnerable to that kind of defense: he's been MORE efficient against the blitz (78.3% completion, +1.378 EPA/play, 73.9% success rate across 23 snaps) than against a standard four-man rush (70.0% completion, +0.323 EPA/play, 58.3% success rate across 60 snaps) — the opposite of the typical pattern. This only surfaces by directly cross-referencing Denver's takeaway-heavy defensive identity against Purdy's own blitz-specific splits; neither fact alone tells this story. What changes: if Denver leans on the extra-rusher pressure that has generated its league-best turnover rate against the rest of its schedule, Purdy's season-long evidence says that's precisely the look he's already handled best, not worst. Confidence: MEDIUM (the underlying splits for both sides are real and specific, but Denver's actual blitz-rate tendency for this specific matchup was not available to confirm how often it's likely to be deployed here).

**Finding 3: McCaffrey doesn't need to be at his most explosive for this matchup to produce an explosive game.** McCaffrey's early-season rushing line (55.3 yds/gm) has leaned on blocking more than individual explosiveness — 2.85 yards before contact per carry against just 1.70 after contact, with only 1.0 broken tackles per game, a different profile than his career-level burst. Denver's run defense is specifically broken in exactly the category his current profile hasn't needed to lean on: 29th in 20+ yard runs allowed to backs, 30th in 40+ yard runs allowed, and 31st overall in rush yards allowed to running backs (129.7/gm). Context Expansion: McCaffrey's own contextual tier splits show all three of his 2026 opponents graded mid-tier against the run (LAR 11th, MIA 18th, ARI 21st) — he has not yet faced a defense this generous against the run in 2026, which means this is a genuine step up in opportunity independent of whatever form he's shown so far. Confidence: MEDIUM-HIGH.

## 7. COEUS FINAL READ

### Keys to the Game

**If San Francisco's offense is allowed to operate in its preferred rhythm** — a 63.0% third-down conversion rate (1st in the league) against a Denver defense allowing 47.8% (27th) — this game is likely to feature long, sustained SF possessions that limit Denver's own opportunities, regardless of how Denver's defense performs on any individual snap.

**If Denver's defense generates the kind of pressure that has produced its league-leading takeaway rate,** the evidence says to watch HOW it gets there — extra rushers specifically are the one look Purdy's own season-long splits say he's handled better than a standard rush, which is a real tension between Denver's season-long defensive success and this week's specific opponent.

**If McCaffrey sees even a modest workload increase inside the red zone (already a 61.5% carry share there) against a defense this broken against explosive runs,** that's the single likeliest source of a game-changing play in either direction.

**If Denver's offense is forced to lean on the pass inside the red zone** — its natural tendency (62.5% pass rate there) — against a San Francisco defense that specifically tightens against the pass in that area (43.6% allowed, 25th) while inviting the run, expect stalled Denver red-zone trips to be a recurring theme rather than a one-off.

### The Verdict

The broad thesis from the Pregame Briefing — that San Francisco holds the structural edge almost everywhere except the turnover column — holds up under the deeper look. San Francisco's situational efficiency (third down, red zone scheme fit, home performance) and its offensive weapons (Purdy, McCaffrey, Kittle, Evans) are stacked against specific, documented Denver weaknesses (run defense, red-zone identity, 24th-ranked pass defense), while Denver's one legitimate counter — its league-best takeaway rate — runs into a quarterback whose own numbers say he's uniquely unlikely to be rattled by exactly that kind of pressure. None of this guarantees an outcome; a three-game sample on both sides leaves real uncertainty, and if Denver's defense creates even one or two live-ball turnovers, the math changes quickly. But on the evidence available, San Francisco profiles as the significantly better-positioned team in nearly every phase of this specific matchup, with Denver's path running almost entirely through its defense rather than its offense.
