## 1. PREGAME BRIEFING

This is an early kickoff (9:30 AM ET), consistent with an NFL International Series window — worth noting as scheduling context, though nothing in the evidence here speaks to how either team has performed in that window before.

Pittsburgh enters at 4-2, having won its last two games (34-22 over Indianapolis, 26-19 over Tampa Bay) after a Week 4 loss to Cleveland. The Steelers' profile so far is built on balance rather than a single dominant unit: a top-half passing offense (247.8 pass yds/gm, 12th), a defense that ranks top-10 in points allowed (21.3/gm, 12th) and pass yards allowed (222.0/gm, 8th), and a running game that is modest by raw output (99.2 rush yds/gm, 20th) but has been efficient in small samples.

New Orleans enters at 2-4, and the record undersells how competitive its offense has actually been — 25.3 points/gm ranks 10th in the league, ahead of Pittsburgh's own scoring output (22.8, 17th). The problem has been almost entirely on defense: New Orleans ranks 32nd in points allowed (29.7/gm) — the worst mark in the NFL — 30th in total yards allowed, 30th in rush yards allowed, and 26th in pass yards allowed. This is a defense that has been generationally bad in 2026 regardless of opponent, which shapes almost everything about how this game should be expected to play out.

These two teams have not met yet this season, so there is no head-to-head form to draw on. The broad shape of this matchup, before getting into the specific levers that will decide it: Pittsburgh's offense — a competent, veteran-quarterbacked unit — is walking into a matchup against the league's worst defense by scoring average, while New Orleans' own offense, which has shown real life particularly through the air, must sustain drives against a Pittsburgh defense that is considerably more disciplined than anything it has faced in recent weeks. On paper, this profiles as a game where Pittsburgh's floor is high and New Orleans' offense will need to play close to its ceiling just to keep pace. What determines whether that turns into a comfortable Pittsburgh win or a shootout is worked through below.

## 5. THREAT INTELLIGENCE

Football Intel's Threat Engine is deterministic: a Threat fires only when a player's own season rank in a specific statistical category AND the upcoming opponent's defensive rank in that same category both clear a fixed threshold — not either signal alone, but their convergence. The three tiers use exact thresholds (player rank is 1st-best; defense rank is 1st-toughest, so a high defense-rank number means a weak defense): **Nuclear** requires the player to rank top 3 AND the opponent to rank 30th or worse in that category; **Elite** requires top 5 AND opponent 28th or worse; **Standard** requires top 10 AND opponent 23rd or worse. A "Double" designation means one category met both thresholds; "Triple" adds a second category where the player's own rank alone clears the bar even without the defense side converging.

**Aaron Rodgers (PIT) — Standard, Double (pass_tds).** Rodgers ranks 7th in the league in pass touchdowns this season, and New Orleans ranks 23rd in pass touchdowns allowed — both clearing the Standard threshold. His pass-yardage category did not converge into a Threat despite New Orleans' weak overall pass defense (26th), because Rodgers' own pass-yardage rank (11th) falls just outside the top-10 cutoff the system requires. Context from elsewhere in this report complicates the designation somewhat: Rodgers' own splits show real suppression against pressure (see Matchup Intelligence), and his career-long touchdown total being matchup-driven rather than built on elite protection is a real source of game-to-game variance. This is a season-long statistical convergence, not a guarantee — treat it as a real, data-backed signal rather than an unqualified edge. Confidence: MEDIUM.

**Jaylen Warren (PIT) — Standard, Triple (rush_yds primary; rec_yds and rec as extra categories).** Warren ranks 10th in the league in rush yards among backs, and New Orleans ranks 32nd in rush yards allowed to backs — the single most extreme convergence in this report, clearing the Standard threshold by a wide margin. His rec_yds (2nd) and rec (9th) categories count as "extra" under the Triple designation because his own individual ranks clear the top-10 bar even though New Orleans' defense isn't specifically weak against receiving backs in those exact categories (13th and 14th, respectively — short of the 23+ threshold needed for those categories to converge on their own). As detailed in Matchup Intelligence, this Threat is backed by a genuinely extreme opponent-quality gap, though it's worth repeating that Warren himself has zero games this season against a true bottom-tier run defense, so there's no in-season precedent for exactly how large this edge plays out. Confidence: MEDIUM-HIGH on the matchup quality, MEDIUM overall.

**No Threat designations fired for any New Orleans player this week.** Chris Olave is the clearest case worth explaining: his own season ranks are elite (3rd in rec yds, 5th in receptions), but Pittsburgh's pass defense doesn't rank weak enough in either category (14th in rec yds allowed to WRs, 18th in receptions allowed) to clear even the Standard threshold of 23rd-or-worse. This is a real individual talent facing a defense that, while not elite, isn't bad enough in the specific categories the system screens for — a genuine distinction between "good player" and "system-flagged mismatch." The absence of a Threat does not mean the absence of a real angle (see Man vs. Zone above for the team-level passing-game case on Pittsburgh's side); it means this specific convergence wasn't met this week.

## 6. HIDDEN INTELLIGENCE & CONTEXTUAL ANALYSIS

**Finding 1: Pittsburgh's extreme pass-heavy red-zone identity concentrates its scoring risk in a tight end room that happens to be facing the league's most exploitable tight end defense.** Pittsburgh passes on 76.1% of its red-zone plays — the highest rate in the NFL — while running on just 23.9% of them, the lowest rate in the league; its running backs have scored just 1 rushing touchdown all season (31st). That pass-heavy identity funnels disproportionately into the tight end room: Pat Freiermuth has converted 4 touchdowns on just 5 red-zone targets this season (a 23.3% red-zone target share), while the team's tight ends overall have combined for 6 touchdowns, 4th-most in the league. New Orleans' defense, meanwhile, has allowed the most tight end targets of any team in the NFL (31st) and ranks 28th in tight end receiving yards allowed, while also facing an unusually high volume of red-zone snaps overall (13.0/gm, well above a typical defense). The reason this isn't obvious: a reader looking only at Pittsburgh's modest raw tight end receiving numbers (Freiermuth 30.0 rec yds/gm, Washington 26.0) would miss that the real value here isn't yardage — it's scoring concentration, created by a specific play-calling identity meeting a defense that has shown it cannot defend that exact situation. What changes because of it: the clearest scoring path in this game for Pittsburgh may run less through Metcalf's volume and more through a tight end red-zone look, a detail easy to miss focused only on season-long receiving totals. Confidence: MEDIUM (the underlying ranks are real and specific; the causal "funneling" read connecting them is a reasonable inference, not a directly labeled data point).

**Finding 2: Jaylen Warren's rushing production is tied to Aaron Rodgers' passing success, not an inverse run-funnel relationship — which complicates how automatically New Orleans' historically bad run defense should translate into a bigger workload for Warren.** Football Intel's own cross-statistic data shows a striking correlation: when Rodgers has a representative passing game, Warren hits his own rushing average 60.0% of the time (n=10); when Rodgers is below his own average, that rate falls to just 11.1% (n=9). The relationship runs just as strongly in the other direction — when Warren's rushing is hot, Rodgers hits his own passing average 85.7% of the time (n=7), compared to only 33.3% when Warren is cold (n=12). This is the opposite of what a simple "bad run defense means lean into the run" read would predict; it suggests Pittsburgh's offense functions as a connected whole (both units clicking together in competitive, on-schedule football) rather than a run/pass seesaw that reacts to opponent weakness. That matters directly against New Orleans: Pittsburgh's rushing production has stayed remarkably flat across opponent tiers all season (92.7 rush yds/gm vs. top-tier run defenses, 93.3 vs. mid-tier — almost no variance), which argues Pittsburgh's own scheme isn't likely to suddenly expand Warren's workload just because the matchup allows it. Layer in Rodgers' documented vulnerability under pressure (-0.342 EPA/play blitzed vs. -0.034 unblitzed) and Pittsburgh's bottom-5 sack rate allowed (29th) — if New Orleans' defense wants to limit this offense's overall efficiency, pressuring Rodgers may be a higher-leverage lever than loading the box against Warren, even though Warren's own matchup-based Threat designation is real. Confidence: LOW-MEDIUM (the hit-rate samples are real but modest, n=7-14 per cell), but this is a genuine, non-obvious tension between two real evidence threads rather than a restatement of either alone.

**Finding 3: Chris Olave's actual route profile largely sidesteps Pittsburgh's biggest defensive strength of the moment.** Pittsburgh's slot defense has been the best in the league over the last three games (1st in both slot yards/gm and yards per target allowed, per PFF), a significant recent improvement on an already strong season-long mark (7th). But Olave runs the overwhelming majority of his routes from the perimeter (66.9% wide alignment, just 32.7% slot per PFF) — meaning the defense's sharpest current strength mostly doesn't apply to New Orleans' best and most heavily-targeted receiver. Instead, Olave is more likely to see boundary coverage from Asante Samuel Jr., who has allowed a 106.9 QB rating and 3 touchdowns on just 20 targets this season — Pittsburgh's clearest individual coverage weakness.
