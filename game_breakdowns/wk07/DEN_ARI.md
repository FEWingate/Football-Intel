# GAME BREAKDOWN: Denver Broncos @ Arizona Cardinals
### Week 7, 2026 Season | 10/25/2026, 4:05 PM ET

## 1. PREGAME BRIEFING

Denver arrives at 4-2, built on a defense that has kept the scoreboard respectable (20.8 points allowed per game, 10th in the NFL) despite genuinely poor underlying yardage numbers — the Broncos rank 26th in total yards allowed per game (369.8) and dead last in the bottom tier against the run, 31st at 139.8 rush yds/gm allowed. The offense has been even more lopsided: Denver ranks 31st in total yards (259.0/gm) and 30th in passing (174.8 pass yds/gm), with a wide receiver corps that has produced the league's worst receiving output at the position (97.8 rec yds/gm, 32nd). Denver's two losses (at Kansas City, at San Francisco) came against two of its toughest matchups; its four wins have mostly been low-scoring, field-position affairs (17-14 over Seattle, 20-13 over Jacksonville).

Arizona sits at 2-4, and the record undersells how competitive several of those losses were — a 36-30 shootout loss at San Francisco, an 18-16 win at the Rams, a 21-45 blowout loss to Detroit sandwiched in between. The offense has real juice at receiver and tight end (Trey McBride and Michael Wilson have combined for over half of Arizona's air yards most weeks) but the defense has been one of the most beatable units in the league by the scoreboard, allowing a league-worst 16 passing touchdowns (32nd) and ranking 31st in points allowed (29.7/gm).

These two teams have not played each other this season, and this is not a division matchup, so there is no head-to-head form line to lean on. The broad shape of this game is worth stating plainly before digging into the details: this profiles as a matchup between an offense that can't stretch the field through the air (Denver) against a defense that gives up explosive passing plays as readily as any in the league (Arizona), paired with a run-funneling Denver defense facing an Arizona backfield currently clouded by a real injury question at the top of the depth chart. Which of those storylines actually decides the game is the subject of the rest of this report.

## 4. MATCHUP INTELLIGENCE

**Bo Nix (DEN).** Nix has started all 6 games, averaging 174.8 pass yds/gm (30th) on a 60.3% completion rate (25th) with 6 touchdowns (25th) and a low 0.5 INT/gm (10th) — a low-mistake, low-ceiling profile. His opponent-quality splits are extreme: 141.3 pass yds/gm against top-tier pass defenses (n=3) compared to 288.0 yds/gm in his one game against a bottom-tier unit (Jacksonville, Week 2, which ranked 31st against the pass that week). Arizona's pass defense (247.3 yds/gm allowed, 23rd, a bottom-tier unit) is the closest thing on Denver's schedule to that Jacksonville matchup. On the road specifically, Nix has actually been worse than his season average (156.0 pass yds/gm across 3 road games vs. 174.8 overall), and his road-and-tier combination against a bottom-tier pass defense doesn't yet exist in his career sample — his only bottom-tier game came at home. Confidence: LOW-MEDIUM. The matchup quality favors a big game on paper, but the lone supporting data point is a single, non-road sample.

**Jacoby Brissett (ARI).** Brissett has started all 6 games, averaging 221.3 pass yds/gm (21st) on a strong 66.7% completion rate (7th) with 9 touchdowns (16th), playing behind protection that has allowed 2.0 sacks/gm (11th) and taking 1.0 INT/gm (24th). At home, his numbers dip to 193.5 pass yds/gm across 2 games. Confidence: MEDIUM. The completion-rate profile and protection quality both argue for a competent floor even without a home/mid-tier-specific data point.

Both quarterbacks have shown real, well-supported vulnerability to the blitz, independent of this specific matchup: Nix's completion rate falls from 64.7% (unblitzed, n=133) to 42.4% (blitzed, n=66), with EPA/play swinging from +0.02 to -0.266. ### Man vs. Zone (Required)

**Denver offense vs. Arizona defense.** Arizona's defense plays zone on 80.3% of its coverage snaps (man rate ranks 27th-most, i.e., one of the leaguer's more zone-heavy units per PFF), and it has been mediocre in both shells: 8.03 yards/target allowed in zone (ranked 20th) and an even worse 7.92 yards/target allowed in man (ranked 27th, with 8 touchdowns surrendered on just 38 man-coverage targets — a notably high rate). Denver's own passing attack, per PFF team-level charting, has graded essentially the same regardless of shell (6.98 ypt vs. zone, 26th; 6.75 ypt vs. man, 18th) on a passing attack that plays man only 25.0% of the time it's charted. The two don't create a clean mismatch in either direction — PFF's own weighted projection for this pairing comes back at 6.93 yards/target, "about neutral" compared to Denver's unweighted 6.92 average. Individually, Waddle trends toward man for explosiveness (15.6 yds/reception) and Sutton trends toward zone for efficiency (12.6 yds/reception, more route volume) — against a defense that leans zone by a wide margin, Sutton's profile is the better schematic fit even if Waddle carries the bigger overall role.

**Arizona offense vs. Denver defense.** This is the more lopsided individual pairing. Denver's defense plays man on 28.1% of coverage snaps (13th-most man-heavy in the league per PFF) and is drastically split by shell: an elite 5.84 yards/target allowed in zone (ranked 1st in the NFL, with 6 interceptions on 160 zone targets) against a poor 8.21 yards/target allowed in man (ranked 28th, 6 touchdowns surrendered on just 72 man targets). Arizona's own passing attack mirrors that split almost perfectly: an excellent 8.6 yards/target vs. zone (6th in the league, 98.5 passer rating) against a league-worst 4.83 yards/target vs. man (32nd, though a notably high 9.9% touchdown rate in man — likely a product of Arizona's heavy red-zone pass-calling, where man coverage is more common). Because Denver plays zone on nearly three-quarters of its snaps — exactly the shell where it is a top-ranked unit and exactly the shell McBride feeds off of — this sets up a genuine collision rather than a clean advantage for either side. PFF's weighted projection for this pairing (7.54 ypt) actually comes back close to Arizona's unweighted average (7.56), reading as "about neutral" overall — but that blended neutral figure is masking the much sharper individual-player tension described above and in Hidden Intelligence below.

## 5. THREAT INTELLIGENCE

A Threat designation is generated by Football Intel's deterministic Threat Engine, not by Coeus. It fires when a player's own SEASON rank in a specific statistical category and the upcoming opponent's defensive rank in that SAME category both clear a fixed threshold — the convergence of genuinely elite individual production and a genuinely weak specific matchup, not either signal in isolation. The three tiers use these exact thresholds (player rank is 1st-best; defense rank is 1st-toughest, so a high defense-rank number means a weak unit):

- **Nuclear** — player ranks top 3 in the category AND the opponent's defense ranks 30th or worse in that same category
- **Elite** — player ranks top 5 AND opponent defense ranks 28th or worse
- **Standard** — player ranks top 10 AND opponent defense ranks 23rd or worse

Double/Triple/Quadruple reflects how many categories converged for a given player.

Every listed starter for both teams was evaluated against this week's specific opponent — Bo Nix, J.K. Dobbins, Courtland Sutton, Jaylen Waddle and Evan Engram for Denver; Jacoby Brissett, Jeremiyah Love, Michael Wilson, Kendrick Bourne and Trey McBride for Arizona — and **no Threat designation fired for any player on either side of this matchup.** That reflects this specific convergence system's thresholds not being met across these specific player/opponent category pairs; it does not mean the matchup lacks real edges. Several of the findings above (Engram's bottom-tier TE matchup, the McBride/zone-vs-man tension, Arizona's league-worst passing-touchdowns-allowed rate) are genuine, evidence-backed angles that simply don't happen to clear this particular deterministic bar this week.

## 6. HIDDEN INTELLIGENCE & CONTEXTUAL ANALYSIS

**Finding 1: Denver's passing and rushing success are tightly linked — this is not a game where one phase can be expected to carry the other if it struggles.** J.K. Dobbins has posted an above-average rushing game in 80% of contests where Bo Nix also had an above-average passing game (n=5 of a possible 8 such pairings), compared to just 36.4% when Nix was below his own average (n=4 of 11). The relationship runs both directions: Nix himself has posted a hit-rate game 50% of the time when Dobbins also ran well (n=4 of 8), compared to just 12.5% when Dobbins struggled (n=1 of 8). This is not an obvious inverse (run more when the pass isn't working) relationship — it's a genuine co-movement, consistent with Denver's offense functioning as a connected whole rather than two independently alternating levers. Context Expansion: Nix's own opponent-quality splits show real suppression against tougher defenses (141.3 pass yds/gm vs. top-tier pass defenses, n=3) and real inflation against weak ones (288.0 yds/gm vs. the one bottom-tier defense he's faced). Arizona's pass defense this week (247.3 yds/gm allowed, 23rd, a bottom-tier unit) is a plausible trigger for Nix's better version — and if that holds, the correlation above says Dobbins' ground game (facing a mid-tier Arizona run defense, 20th) is more likely to come along for the ride than to serve as a fallback plan if the passing game stalls. Confidence: LOW-MEDIUM — the underlying samples (n=8-14 games) are real but modest, and the mechanism (game-script correlation vs. pure coincidence) is inferred rather than directly labeled.

**Finding 2: Arizona's two most heavily used receiving weapons are both primary slot players who specifically depend on zone coverage — and Denver's defense has quietly become one of the league's best units against the slot over its last three games, at the same time it already ranks as a top-tier zone defense overall.** Trey McBride (64% slot rate, 30.2% target share) and Kendrick Bourne (69.2% slot rate, 13.9% target share) combine for nearly half of Arizona's passing-game usage, and both players' own production collapses outside of zone coverage specifically (McBride: 88.9% catch rate and 8.4 yds/reception vs. zone, falling to 53.8% and 5.0 vs. man). Denver's slot defense, which ranked a mediocre 21st in yards/gm and 24th in yards/target allowed over the full season, has sharply tightened over its last three games to 7th in yards/gm and 5th in yards/target — while Denver's defense overall already ranks 1st in the NFL in yards/target allowed against zone coverage specifically (5.84 ypt, per PFF). Context Expansion: this recent-form slot improvement is not reflected anywhere in Denver's season-long team defense numbers used elsewhere in this report (which still show a middling overall pass defense, 230.0 yds/gm allowed, 16th) — a reader looking only at the season-long team stat would miss that the specific coverage look Arizona's top two weapons depend on has been trending the wrong way for them over the past month. Confidence: MEDIUM — the recent-form sample is real and sizable (3 games) and the mechanism (slot-heavy usage colliding with an improving slot defense) is directly supported, though three games is still a limited window for projecting forward.

**Finding 3: Arizona's offense is drastically more TD-efficient against man coverage than its yardage numbers would suggest, and that pattern lines up specifically with its red-zone identity — which matters because Denver's defense, when it does play man, has been one of the league's leakiest units in exactly that situation.** Arizona's team passing attack posts a league-worst 4.83 yards/target against man coverage (32nd per PFF) — yet produces a 9.9% touchdown rate on those same man-coverage targets, more than four times its zone touchdown rate (2.2%). That combination (poor yardage efficiency, elevated touchdown rate) is the signature of a red-zone-weighted sample rather than an open-field one, and it fits: Arizona already calls the 4th-most pass-heavy red-zone offense in the league (61.0% pass rate inside the 20). Denver's defense, meanwhile, has allowed 6 touchdowns on just 72 man-coverage targets all season (an 8.3% rate) despite playing man relatively rarely (28.1% of snaps) — a real, specific leak in exactly the coverage shell Arizona's offense is built to punish near the goal line. Context Expansion: Arizona's red-zone play-calling data (39.0% run / 61.0% pass, both extreme ranks) and Denver's red-zone defensive tendency (48.9% pass-snaps allowed, 23rd — opponents throw against Denver in the red zone more than average) both point the same direction independently of the coverage-shell data, reinforcing rather than merely coinciding with it. Confidence: MEDIUM — the individual data points (TD rate by shell, red-zone pass rate, red-zone defensive tendency) are all real and specific, though the causal connection between them (that these man-coverage touchdowns specifically occur in the red zone) is an inference from the pattern rather than a directly labeled red-zone-coverage split.
