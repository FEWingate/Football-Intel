## 1. PREGAME BRIEFING

Miami arrives at 0-5, the only winless team remaining in the AFC East, having lost every one of its first five games by a margin ranging from 2 to 22 points. New York is 2-4, having won two of its last five after an 0-1 start, with both wins (Week 1 over Tennessee, Week 5 over Cleveland) coming against teams currently near the bottom of their own divisions. This is a divisional game (AFC East), but the two teams have not yet met this season — there is no 2026 head-to-head result to draw from, and a scan of both rosters' game logs turns up no player on either side with a logged game against this specific opponent yet this year. Whatever familiarity exists between these franchises from prior seasons isn't captured in this evidence set, so this preview leans entirely on each team's 2026 body of work.

The broad shape of this game is defined by a mismatch of identities rather than a mismatch of talent. Miami's offense is one of the least productive passing attacks in the league but has quietly been an effective rushing offense all year. New York's defense is built around taking away exactly the thing Miami can't do anyway (throw the ball) while being considerably more exposed against exactly the thing Miami can do (run it). Both teams' defenses have off-setting flaws of their own. The broad expectation here is a lower-scoring, ground-and-pound framework in which whichever offense can stay ahead of the chains on early downs — and whichever defense can get off the field on third down — controls the game. The deeper layers of exactly how that's likely to play out are developed below.

The one offensive bright spot is the ground game: 126.6 rush yds/gm ranks 9th (top 10) in the league, a genuinely incongruous number for an 0-5 team with this profile.

Down/distance: Miami actually converts third downs at a respectable 41.3% clip (12th) on 12.6 attempts/gm — the offense isn't dying on third down, it's dying on early downs and in the passing game broadly. Red zone play-calling is the most extreme split in this report: once inside the 20, Miami runs the ball 66.7% of the time, the single highest red-zone run rate in the NFL (1st), and throws just 33.3% of the time (worst pass-rate in the league). This is a pure tendency stat, not a quality one, but it's an emphatic one — Miami goes to the ground almost exclusively near the goal line.

Contextual splits (team_context, opponent-quality tiers): Miami's scoring shrinks hard against good defenses — 11.0 points/gm against top-tier defensive matchups (n=3) versus 24.0 (n=1) and 13.0 (n=1) against mid- and bottom-tier units, though the mid/bottom samples are a single game apiece. ## 4. MATCHUP INTELLIGENCE

### Miami's Passing Attack vs. New York's Pass Defense

The headline number here needs no embellishment: Miami's passing offense (169.8 yds/gm, 31st, bottom 3; 56.2% completion, league-worst) is walking into a defense that ranks 5th (top 5) against the pass. There is almost no realistic path for this to be a good night through the air for Miami.

**Malik Willis (MIA, QB1, depth chart pos_rank 1)** has started all five games and is averaging 169.8 pass yds/gm with just 2 touchdowns against 0.8 INT/gm — an efficient-looking 56.2% completion rate that still ranks dead last in the league given the lack of explosive production (11.6 yards per completion, a top-12 number, suggesting the few completions he does complete travel further than most, but there simply aren't enough of them). On the road specifically, that number narrows to 167.3 pass yds/gm (3 games, 79.3 rating, 3.7 sacks/gm absorbed). In his one road start against a pass defense in the same class as New York's (top-10, 5th-ranked), he was held to just 85 yards on 17 attempts — Week 4 at Minnesota's then-2nd-ranked pass defense, 52.9% completion, zero touchdowns. That's a small sample (n=1) but it's the single most relevant data point available, and it points toward another suppressed outing. Willis's own splits against pressure make this worse: a 44.7% completion rate and -0.427 EPA/play when blitzed, against an already-poor -0.126 EPA/play when not blitzed — and New York's front generates real pressure (2.8 sacks/gm, 8th, top 10, team-wide), a legitimately bad pairing for Miami's protection, which already surrenders 2.8 sacks/gm itself (25th).

**Geno Smith (NYJ, QB1)** is averaging 222.7 pass yds/gm (20th) with a strong 68.9% completion rate (4th, top 10) but a concerning 3.2 sacks/gm absorbed (28th, bottom 10). At home, his production actually improves to 214.5 pass yds/gm (2 games, 88.3 rating). Against a pass defense in the same tier Miami currently occupies (12th, "mid" by this evidence's own tiering), his one home sample came in Week 5 vs. a similarly mid-tier Cleveland defense (15th): 182 yards, 56.7% completion, 1 touchdown. Smith has not yet faced a true top-10 pass defense at home this season, so there's no exact precedent for an elite shutdown unit — but Miami's pass defense (12th, not elite) does not project as one, which is a point in Smith's favor given his own home numbers trend up, not down.

### Man vs. Zone (PFF, 2026 season-to-date)

**Miami's defense vs. New York's offense.** Miami plays man coverage on just 29.2% of its coverage snaps (zone 70.8%), the league's 12th-most-man mix, per PFF. The split in what it allows is dramatic: 8.52 yards per target vs. man coverage (31st, bottom 3, nearly the worst mark in the league) compared to a much more respectable 7.63 yards per target in zone (15th). New York's passing offense, per PFF receiver charting, has been excellent specifically against man coverage — a 124.2 team passer rating (six touchdowns, zero interceptions on 55 man-coverage targets) compared to 87.0 in zone (five interceptions on 168 targets). Garrett Wilson personally drives much of that man-coverage number: 92.9% catch rate, 3 touchdowns, and a 154.5 targeted quarterback rating on his 14 man-coverage targets this season, against a far more modest 66.7% catch rate and zero touchdowns on 30 zone targets. The blended projection (projected_vs_this_defense) for New York's passing offense against Miami's actual coverage mix reads "about neutral" (7.69 overall yards/target vs. 7.68 weighted for Miami's shell mix) — because Miami is mostly a zone defense and only sees 29.2% man — but any snap where Miami does play man, particularly with Wilson matched up, is a real mismatch given how far Miami's man coverage trails its zone coverage.

**New York's defense vs. Miami's offense.** New York plays man on 32.0% of coverage snaps (8th-most, per PFF) and defends it well — 5.49 yards per target allowed vs. man (9th, top 10), its better shell, against 7.31 in zone (11th). Miami's passing offense, by contrast, has been actively bad against man coverage all season: a 43.8 team passer rating with 3 interceptions on just 34 man-coverage targets, compared to a more passable 78.5 rating in zone. The projection again reads "about neutral" overall (7.72 vs. 7.64 weighted), but the man-coverage shell specifically — the one New York plays more often than most defenses — is the single worst matchup split in this entire report for either offense: Miami's passing attack against New York's better, more-frequently-played coverage look.

### Miami's Run Game vs. New York's Run Defense

This is the matchup both teams' broader profiles point toward, and the contact-efficiency detail supports leaning on it. **Ollie Gordon II (MIA, RB1, depth chart pos_rank 1)** has emerged as Miami's clear lead back — 59.0 rush yds/gm across 4 games, a 42.3% rush share, and a dominant 57.7% share of red-zone carries, fitting neatly with Miami's extreme red-zone run tendency. His underlying efficiency is real: 3.04 yards before contact and 1.98 yards after contact per carry, with 1.25 broken tackles/gm. On the road, he's averaging 53.5 rush yds/gm (2 games, 8.9 yards per carry); he hasn't yet faced a bottom-tier run defense on the road this season, so there's no exact precedent for this week's specifically favorable matchup, but his 100-yard, 11.1-yards-per-carry outing against a top-tier run defense in Week 4 (vs. Minnesota's then-3rd-ranked run defense) suggests he does not need a soft matchup to produce. New York's run defense (24th, bottom 10 team-wide; 27th, bottom 10, allowing production specifically to backs) is a clear step down in difficulty from that Minnesota performance, and New York's own defense has already allowed 190.0 rush yds/gm (n=2) against top-tier rushing offenses this season — the exact profile Miami brings.

**De'Von Achane** was Miami's most featured back in Weeks 1-2 (season rush_share 44.2%, target_share 14.1% in limited action) but has not appeared in the team's game log since Week 3 despite no injury designation on file, and now sits at RB4 on the current depth chart (dated 2026-10-21) — a real, notable drop that isn't explained by anything in this evidence beyond his absence from recent usage. Treat Gordon, not Achane, as the clear workload leader into this game.

**Braelon Allen (NYJ, RB, depth chart pos_rank 2)** has become New York's de facto starter over the past three games (100% rush share Week 4, 75% Week 5, 63% Week 6), posting 52.3 rush yds/gm across all six games with modest contact numbers (2.19 yards before contact, 2.05 after, 1.0 broken tackles/gm). At home specifically, he's actually been better — 61.5 rush yds/gm (2 games, 4.2 yards per carry, a rushing touchdown in each) — and both of those home games came against run defenses ranked 19th and 22nd (Green Bay, Cleveland), a very similar tier to Miami's 16th-ranked unit this week, a genuinely favorable precedent. **Breece Hall**, by contrast, is still listed at RB1 on the current depth chart despite not having logged a game since Week 3, where he was averaging 54.3 rush yds/gm with real receiving involvement (34.0 rec yds/gm) across his three starts, including notably high broken-tackle production (1.67/gm). With no injury designation on file for either back, there is a real, same-week uncertainty about who the Jets actually lean on in the backfield — this report treats Allen as the more likely lead role-holder given three straight weeks of clear usage, at LOW-to-MEDIUM confidence, while noting Hall's underlying per-touch profile (when healthy) is the stronger one if he is in fact active and featured.

## 5. THREAT INTELLIGENCE

Football Intel's deterministic Threat Engine flags a player when their own SEASON-long rank in a specific statistical category AND the upcoming opponent's defensive rank in that SAME category both clear a tier's exact threshold at the same time — the convergence of individually elite production and a specifically weak matchup in that one category, not either signal alone. The three tiers use fixed thresholds (player rank is 1st-best; defense rank is 1st-toughest, so a high defense-rank number means a weak defense): **Nuclear** requires the player to rank top 3 in the category AND the opponent's defense to rank 30th or worse in that same category; **Elite** requires top 5 AND opponent defense 28th or worse; **Standard** requires top 10 AND opponent defense 23rd or worse. "Double," "Triple," and "Quadruple" labels describe how many categories converged for that player, not a different threshold.

No Threat designation fired for any evaluated starter on either roster this week — Malik Willis, Ollie Gordon II, Malik Washington, Chris Bell, and Greg Dulcich for Miami; Geno Smith, Braelon Allen, Garrett Wilson, Omar Cooper Jr., and Mason Taylor for New York. The reasons are instructive rather than arbitrary. On Miami's side, no offensive player clears even the Standard top-10 individual-rank threshold in a meaningful category this season — Willis ranks outside the top 30 in both pass yards and touchdowns, Washington sits in the low-30s in receiving yards, and Gordon's committee usage has kept his individual rank data unpopulated in this system. On New York's side, the closer call belongs to Garrett Wilson: he faces a Miami pass defense that is extremely stingy against wide receivers specifically (3rd, top 5, in rec yds/gm allowed) — the wrong direction entirely for a Threat, which needs a WEAK opponent rank (23rd or worse), not a strong one. Wilson's own season rank (19th in rec yds/gm) also falls short of even the Standard top-10 threshold regardless. In short: the absence of designations here does not mean the absence of real matchup angles — Section 4 above identifies several (Wilson vs. man coverage, the tight end funnel, the run-defense volume gap) — it means none of this specific convergence system's exact thresholds were met for either roster this week.

## 6. HIDDEN INTELLIGENCE & CONTEXTUAL ANALYSIS

**Finding 1: New York's defense is quietly built to force exactly the game plan Miami already wants to run.** New York's pass defense ranks 5th (top 5) in the league and is above-average against all three pass-catching positions (9th vs. WRs, 11th vs. RBs, 7th vs. TEs), but its run defense ranks 24th (bottom 10) overall and 27th (bottom 10) specifically against running backs. This isn't just a ranking quirk — the Contextual Statistics confirm it's already happening in practice: against top-tier rushing offenses this season, New York's defense has allowed 190.0 rush yds/gm (n=2), more than double its 75.7 rush yds/gm (n=3) allowed against bottom-tier rushing offenses. Miami brings a 9th-ranked (top 10) rushing offense into this game — the exact caliber of opponent that has already given New York's defense its worst showings — while owning the league's worst (32nd) completion rate and 2nd-worst (31st, bottom 3) scoring offense through the air. The natural, data-supported expectation is that Miami leans into the run far more than a neutral game script would otherwise dictate, and that New York's defense, for all its real coverage quality, is specifically live to be exploited there. Confidence: MEDIUM — the opponent-tier split is a small sample (n=2 and n=3), but it's directionally consistent with the full-season positional ranks, not a standalone outlier.

**Finding 2: Both offenses have shown a real "seesaw" where the run and pass rarely both click in the same game — and it shows up on both sides of this specific matchup.** Across Miami's five games, the team's rushing output topped its own average in 3 of 5 games, and in 4 of those 5 games, Malik Willis's passing output moved in the opposite direction of the rushing output that same week (a below-average rushing week paired with an above-average passing week, or vice versa) — most clearly in Week 5 (Cincinnati), where Miami's rush offense posted its best game of the season (217 team rush yards) in the same game Willis posted his worst passing output (137 yards, 9.1 air yards/completion). The same pattern shows up independently in New York's own log: in each of Breece Hall's three starts, his rushing production moved opposite Geno Smith's passing output that week (Hall's one "hit" game, 102 yards in Week 1, paired with Smith's below-average passing day; Hall's two quieter rushing weeks paired with Smith's two biggest passing days). This ties back into Finding 1's Contextual Statistics: Miami's own passing splits already shrink hard against good defenses (144.0 pass yds/gm vs. top-tier matchups, n=3, against 220.0 vs. its one bottom-tier game) — meaning if New York's genuinely elite pass defense suppresses Willis the way its season rank suggests it should, Miami's own history says the rushing attack is more likely, not less, to be the game's deciding factor, reinforcing Finding 1 from a second, independent data angle. Confidence: LOW-MEDIUM — sample sizes are small on both sides (n=3-5 games each), but the pattern is consistent across two unrelated teams' logs rather than a single coincidental stretch.

**Finding 3: Miami's defense has a real, stable leak to backs and tight ends in the passing game that its strong perimeter coverage numbers can obscure.** Miami's pass defense looks merely average on the surface (226.0 pass yds/gm allowed, 12th) and is genuinely elite against wide receivers (110.4 rec yds/gm, 3rd, top 5) — but it ranks 28th (bottom 5) against running backs and 30th (bottom 3) against tight ends in receiving yards allowed per game. The Contextual Statistics show this isn't a garbage-time artifact: Miami's defense has allowed a stable 31-45 rec yds/gm to running backs across every opponent-quality tier it has faced this season (40.7 vs. top-tier offenses, 31.0 vs. mid-tier, 44.0 vs. bottom-tier, n=3/1/1) — a consistent leak regardless of who it's facing, not a spike against one bad opponent. New York's own usage profile is built to exploit exactly this: Mason Taylor and Kenyon Sadiq combine for a real, growing tight-end role (14.8% and 14.3% target shares respectively), and Braelon Allen already carries a real receiving role out of the backfield (7.7% target share, multiple 4-6 target games recently). A reader focused on Miami's genuinely tough WR coverage (3rd in the league) could reasonably — and incorrectly — assume New York's passing attack is walled off; the actual soft spot runs directly through New York's backs and tight ends. Confidence: MEDIUM — the positional ranks and tier splits both point the same direction, though New York's own usage of these outlets has fluctuated week to week.

## 7. COEUS FINAL READ

**Keys to the Game.** If Miami can establish Ollie Gordon II early and lean into its league-leading red-zone run rate (66.7%), it keeps this game in the low-scoring, ball-control framework that gives its historically bad passing offense the fewest opportunities to be exposed — and New York's run defense, which has already allowed 190.0 rush yds/gm to top-tier rushing offenses this season, is a legitimately generous matchup for exactly that plan. If New York's pass rush (2.8 sacks/gm, top 10) gets home against Miami's already-shaky protection (2.8 sacks/gm allowed) and forces Willis into blitz-heavy, obvious passing downs — where his own splits show he completes just 44.7% of throws — Miami's offense could stall entirely regardless of how the ground game is going. And if New York's offense works through Garrett Wilson against man coverage (where Miami is 31st, bottom 3, in yards allowed per target) and through its backs and tight ends underneath (where Miami is bottom-5/bottom-3 at both positions) rather than forcing the ball to the perimeter against Miami's genuinely strong WR coverage (3rd in the league), it should be able to move the ball more easily through the air than New York's modest overall passing rank (20th) would suggest on its own.

**The Verdict.** The broad thesis from the top of this report — a run-heavy, lower-scoring game controlled by whichever side wins the early-down and third-down battles — holds up under the deeper look. Miami's single functional offensive identity (the run) lines up directly against New York's single defensive soft spot (the run), a real and specific advantage buried under Miami's 0-5 record and historically bad overall numbers. But New York's own structural advantages are real too: a legitimately elite pass defense facing the league's worst passing offense, a home red-zone-and-protection edge, and an offense that — while unspectacular overall — has two clean individual levers (Wilson vs. man coverage, the tight end/back funnel) that line up better than Miami's defense's surface-level numbers suggest. Confidence: MEDIUM. This profiles as the kind of game where Miami's rushing attack keeps the final margin competitive without necessarily being enough on its own to overcome New York's larger structural advantages across the rest of the roster.
