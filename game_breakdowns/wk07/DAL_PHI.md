## 3. MATCHUP STATISTICS

### Dallas Offense

## 6. HIDDEN INTELLIGENCE & CONTEXTUAL ANALYSIS

**Finding 1: Philadelphia's own red-zone rushing identity lines up almost exactly with Dallas's single biggest defensive weakness, in a way that isn't visible from either team's full-field numbers alone.** Philadelphia calls designed runs on 63.6% of its red-zone snaps — the 2nd-highest rate in the league — despite being one of the worst rushing offenses in football by full-field yardage (82.2 rush yds/gm, 29th). That tendency isn't obviously supported by Philadelphia's own run-blocking quality elsewhere on the field, but it becomes far more relevant once paired with Dallas's defense: Dallas allows a run on 58.9% of opposing red-zone snaps (3rd-highest in the league) and ranks dead last team-wide against the run (32nd, 149.8 yds/gm allowed) and 25th specifically against backs. The context that makes this more than a coincidence: Dallas's contextual splits show its run defense is specifically vulnerable against good rushing offenses (205.0 rush yards allowed/gm against top-tier rushers it's faced, n=3) but stingy against bad ones (59.5 yds/gm, n=2) — and Philadelphia, whatever its full-field rushing limitations, is not a bad rushing team in short-yardage, goal-line situations by play-calling behavior alone. A offense that already wants to run inside the 20 meeting a defense that specifically can't be trusted to stop the run in that same area is a real, specific point of leverage for Philadelphia regardless of who's at quarterback. Confidence: MEDIUM.

**Finding 2: Dallas's completion-rate defense is deceptively bad relative to its yardage-allowed rank, and the gap is explained by what Philadelphia's defense is doing in reverse.** Dallas ranks 30th in completion percentage allowed (68.7%) — considerably worse than its 20th-ranked pass-yardage number alone suggests — while Philadelphia's defense shows the inverse signature (6th-best completion rate allowed, 60.4%, but only 24th in yards per completion). Dallas's own defense is simply bad at preventing completions in the first place, not merely occasionally surrendering a big play; Philadelphia's defense suppresses completions reasonably well but gives up more on the ones that land. The two profiles argue for different offensive approaches against each defense: against Dallas, high-volume, high-percentage underneath passing concepts should simply work at a higher rate than the surface pass-defense ranking implies; against Philadelphia, the bigger payoff sits with fewer, more aggressive downfield shots rather than short, high-percentage volume. Context Expansion: Dallas's own wide receiver corps already leans into exactly the volume approach that exploits this (1st in the league in targets/gm and receptions/gm), while George Pickens' profile (11.6 average depth of target) is the clearer fit for testing Philadelphia's deeper-completion weakness on the other side. Confidence: MEDIUM — the completion-rate/yards-per-completion split is directly supported, but the prescriptive read on which approach each offense should take is an inference layered on top of it.

**Finding 3: The receiver best positioned by raw usage to exploit Dallas's worst individual coverage weakness (its slot defense) is not the one whose per-target efficiency suggests he'd actually make it pay.** Dallas's slot defense ranks 32nd in the league in yards per target allowed — its single worst individually-ranked unit of any kind in this report — and that number has gotten worse, not better, over the last three games specifically (10.52 yards/target, still 32nd). Philadelphia's clearest slot specialist by role is Makai Lemon (64.1% slot rate, a true primary), but his own route efficiency there has been modest all season (0.64 yards/route run on 26 targets). Dallas Goedert, by contrast, splits time between the slot and inline (39.5%/53.8%) rather than working it primarily, yet is considerably more efficient on a per-target basis overall (76.2 PFF grade, 111.3 passer rating allowed when targeted against zone specifically) — and Dallas's own defense plays zone on 73.6% of its snaps. Context Expansion: Philadelphia's own tight end production splits by opponent quality show real sensitivity to matchup softness (just 21.0 rec yds/gm against top-tier defenses faced, n=3, versus 77.0 in the one bottom-tier game, n=1) — a defense this specifically weak in the slot is closer to that softer end of the spectrum than Philadelphia has typically seen, which argues Goedert's hybrid slot usage, not Lemon's primary one, may be the more productive avenue into this particular weakness if the offensive coordinator chooses to lean into it. Confidence: LOW-MEDIUM — the underlying usage and efficiency numbers are real, but whether Philadelphia's play-calling actually routes more work to Goedert in the slot specifically is not something the evidence can confirm in advance.
