## 5. THREAT INTELLIGENCE

Football Intel's deterministic Threat Engine flags a player when his own season-long rank in a specific statistical category and the upcoming opponent's defensive rank in that same category both clear a fixed threshold at the same time — meaning great individual production is meeting a genuinely weak matchup in that exact category, not just one signal alone. Three tiers, exact thresholds (player rank is 1st-best; defense rank is 1st-toughest, so a high defense-rank number means a weak defense):

- **Nuclear** — player ranks top 3 in the category AND the opponent's defense ranks 30th or worse in that same category
- **Elite** — player ranks top 5 AND opponent defense ranks 28th or worse
- **Standard** — player ranks top 10 AND opponent defense ranks 23rd or worse

"Double/Triple/Quadruple" describes how many categories converged: Double is one category clearing both thresholds; Triple adds a category where the player's own rank alone clears the tier's threshold even without the defense side converging; Quadruple means two or more categories fully converge (both thresholds met).

His rushing (5th) and receiving-yards (4th) ranks are cited as supporting categories (both clear the Elite player-side threshold on their own), which is what makes this a Triple. Separately, **Achane also carries a Standard tier, Double-type designation** built entirely on receiving yards: his own rank (4th) clears Standard's top-10 threshold, and San Francisco's defense ranks 23rd against backs in receiving yards allowed — exactly at Standard's threshold. ## 6. HIDDEN INTELLIGENCE & CONTEXTUAL ANALYSIS

**Finding 1: Miami's run defense isn't just weak on paper — the league is already red-zone-funneling teams directly at it, and San Francisco's own rushing attack has shown it specifically capitalizes on exactly this kind of matchup.** Miami's overall run defense ranked 26th in the NFL in 2025 (132.4 rush yards/game allowed), but the red-zone play-calling data shows something more specific: opponents ran the ball against Miami on 55.7% of their red-zone plays in 2025 — the 4th-highest rate allowed in the league — while passing on just 44.3% (29th-lowest). Offensive coordinators around the league have already identified Miami's run defense as the point of attack once the field shrinks, not just across full games. Cross-referencing this with San Francisco's own opponent-quality splits adds real weight: San Francisco's team rushing production climbed from 92.4 yards/game against top-tier run defenses in 2025 to 129.7 yards/game against bottom-tier run defenses (team_context splits, n=5 and n=6 respectively) — nearly a 40-yard-per-game swing based purely on opponent quality, and running-back-specific rushing followed the identical pattern (77.6 top-tier vs. 117.3 bottom-tier, n=5/n=6). Miami's defense — bottom-third overall and specifically funneled against in the red zone — is a clean fit for San Francisco's bottom-tier profile. This matters directly for the McCaffrey/Black committee: expect meaningfully more rushing volume and red-zone rushing opportunity than San Francisco's modest 106.9 rush yards/game season average alone would suggest, particularly near the goal line. Confidence: MEDIUM — the team-level split sample sizes are real but moderate (n=5-6 per tier), and this is a reasonable inference from two connected but separately-collected evidence categories rather than a single labeled data point.

**Finding 2: Miami's passing game — not its rushing game — actually gets more productive against good defenses, not worse, which is the opposite of what most teams show and points toward a real game-script effect worth watching against a San Francisco offense built to build leads.** Miami's team-level rushing production collapsed against mid-tier defenses in 2025 (67.0 yards/game, n=4) relative to bottom-tier competition (161.5 yards/game, n=8) — an enormous swing consistent with Finding 1. But Miami's wide receiver production moved in the opposite direction: 120.7 receiving yards/game against top-tier defenses in 2025 (n=7) compared to just 101.5 against bottom-tier defenses (n=4) — a genuine inversion, not noise, given the larger top-tier sample. The most plausible explanation is game script: Miami's ground game shuts down against strong opponents, and the offense compensates by throwing more, likely while trailing.
