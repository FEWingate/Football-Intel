## 1. EXECUTIVE DFS SUMMARY

This game is worth attacking, but selectively — it is not a clean "roster the whole slate" shootout script, and it is not a game to avoid outright either. Structurally, Detroit is the far superior offense (28.9 points/game, 3rd, against New Orleans' 18.1, 29th), which argues for weighting salary toward Detroit's skill pieces on name value alone. But the Game Breakdown's own analysis complicates that simple read in three specific, DFS-relevant ways: Jared Goff carries a documented history of suppressed production against pass defenses of exactly New Orleans' caliber; New Orleans' run defense has a specific split that caps the receiving half of Jahmyr Gibbs' game without touching his rushing floor; and Detroit's own secondary has a real, concentrated weakness that gives Chris Olave a legitimate path to outperform his price relative to Amon-Ra St. Brown.

Stack viability is real on both sides, but the two offenses behave differently as correlated units — Detroit's top two weapons rise and fall together, while New Orleans' top two weapons trade off against each other (see Hidden Intelligence). That distinction should drive stack construction more than raw talent evaluation does. Expected popularity concentrates on Detroit's premium pieces (Gibbs, St. Brown) by name and season-long volume; the more interesting leverage in this game sits on New Orleans' side, where Olave's price gap relative to St. Brown looks larger than the underlying evidence justifies. Ceiling potential is real but should be treated as one-sided-favoring-Detroit rather than a true shootout, given New Orleans' documented tendency to lean on one half of its offense rather than both at once.

## 2. FEATURED DFS PLAYS

### PREMIUM TIER ($6,500+)

**Jahmyr Gibbs — RB, DET — $8,000 (16% of a $50,000 cap) — Status: Questionable**
This is the single most expensive player in the game, and the salary is not discounted at all for the Questionable tag — the market is pricing him as a locked-in workhorse. That price buys a genuinely balanced dual-threat role (55.1% rush share, 17.1% target share, 44.4% red-zone touch share) facing a run defense that ranks 26th against backs specifically — a favorable half of his profile. But the other half of that profile runs into a defense that allows the fewest running-back targets in the league and the 4th-fewest running-back receiving yards — a genuinely difficult receiving matchup layered under the price. At $8,000, spending 16% of the cap here typically forecloses pairing him with a second $7,000+ piece without leaning heavily on sub-$3,500 punts elsewhere. Given his name recognition and workhorse role, expect Gibbs to be highly rostered regardless of the injury tag unless it firms up closer to lock — the field tends to anchor to salary and role over matchup-specific coverage splits. **Confidence: MEDIUM** (availability is the swing factor).

**Amon-Ra St. Brown — WR, DET — $7,500 (15% of cap)**
Fair-to-slight-premium pricing for a true 31.3% target-share alpha with real red-zone volume (34.7% red-zone target share). The price does not meaningfully discount for the matchup: New Orleans' pass defense is genuinely elite (4th in yards allowed, 3rd in third-down defense), though St. Brown's own opponent-tier splits (83.8/63.7/97.7 across top/mid/bottom competition) show no clean collapse against good defenses either. This reads as a "pay for the target share" price rather than a matchup-driven discount or premium. Stacking him with Gibbs alone consumes 31% of an entire lineup's budget across two players. Elite name, elite volume, no injury tag — expect St. Brown to be a straightforward public chalk play across formats. **Confidence: MEDIUM-HIGH**, consistent with his Standard-tier Threat designation.

**Chris Olave — WR, NO — $6,700 (13.4% of cap)**
This is the best-priced true alpha receiver in the game. Olave's 29.4% target share is comparable in weight to St. Brown's role, his efficiency is coverage-neutral (no exploitable shell weakness of his own), and his receiving-yardage floor holds up across every opponent tier this season (68.0–92.0 yards/game top/mid/bottom — no collapse against quality defenses). At $800 cheaper than St. Brown, and facing a Detroit secondary with a specific, documented weak point — Amik Robertson has allowed the worst passer rating and most touchdowns of any core defender in this game — this looks underpriced relative to St. Brown on a pure this-game basis. Olave's name recognition is real, but Detroit's flashier offense and win-total narrative plausibly pull ownership away from him and toward St. Brown — the price gap is the leverage angle. **Confidence: MEDIUM-HIGH.**

## 3. HIDDEN INTELLIGENCE

*Selected from the Game Breakdown's own Hidden Intelligence findings — reframed here for what each one actually changes about a lineup decision, not restated.*

**Selected: Finding 1 — Detroit's weapons are additive; New Orleans' are substitutive.**
The Game Breakdown's cross-referenced hit-rate data shows Goff and Gibbs tend to have good games together, while Shough and Kamara tend to trade off — a hot game for one predicts a cold one for the other. **What this changes for a lineup:** it argues against fading Gibbs to fund more Goff-driven passing salary, since betting on Detroit's offense clicking supports rostering both rather than choosing between them. On the New Orleans side, it argues against combining a Shough-centered passing stack (Shough + Olave + Johnson) with a full-priced Kamara in the same lineup — the offense's own documented pattern says Kamara's best rushing games arrive when Shough struggles, not when he's throwing well. A lineup betting on New Orleans' passing game performing should lean toward Estimé or Devin Neal at the backfield slot instead, or knowingly accept the game-script tension. **Confidence: LOW-MEDIUM** — every underlying sample in the Game Breakdown is below 11 games.

**Selected: Finding 2 — New Orleans' run defense caps Gibbs' receiving ceiling without touching his rushing floor.**
New Orleans allows the fewest running-back targets in the league and the 4th-fewest running-back receiving yards, while sitting below average against the run itself (26th against backs). **What this changes for a lineup:** it separates Gibbs' cash-game case from his tournament case. A meaningful share of Gibbs' normal upside comes from receiving work; this specific matchup removes that pathway even in a game script where Detroit's offense is otherwise clicking (per Finding 1). That supports treating Gibbs as a real cash-game floor play on rushing volume alone, while tempering expectations for the PPR-driven ceiling outcomes tournaments need. **Confidence: MEDIUM.**

**Selected: Finding 3 — Detroit's pass-rush numbers mask a coverage weakness concentrated in one defender.**
Detroit generates real pressure (3.0 sacks/game, 4th) and limits completion rate well (6th-best), but ranks 26th in wide receiver yards allowed, 29th in yards per reception allowed, and 30th in wide receiver touchdowns allowed — a gap the Game Breakdown ties specifically to Amik Robertson (96 targets, worst passer rating and touchdown total of any core defender in this game). **What this changes for a lineup:** it's the strongest single piece of evidence behind rostering Olave over what Detroit's overall pass-defense ranking (22nd) alone would suggest, and it argues that Detroit's sack production shouldn't be read as evidence the secondary limits big plays — those are two separate problems for this defense, and only one helps a Lions DST play. **Confidence: MEDIUM** — team splits and Robertson's target volume are season-long; the exact alignment matchup for this game isn't confirmed in the evidence.

## 4. RECOMMENDED STACKS

**Primary Stack — Detroit Onslaught: Jared Goff + Amon-Ra St. Brown (+ Jameson Williams, tournament-only extension)**
Correlation logic: per Finding 1, Detroit's top skill pieces move together rather than trading off, making this a genuinely additive rather than merely convenient stack — St. Brown's target share is intrinsically tied to Goff's volume, and a good Goff day plausibly comes with a good Gibbs day too, supporting a broader Detroit-side build. Matchup support is mixed by design: New Orleans' pass defense is a real, documented obstacle for Goff specifically, so this stack is a bet that Detroit's offense overcomes a tough defensive matchup on talent and volume rather than a bet on a soft matchup. Salary: Goff ($6,000) + St. Brown ($7,500) = $13,500, 27% of cap; adding Jameson Williams ($6,000) for a triple stack pushes to $19,500 (39% of cap) and should be reserved for tournament builds given Williams' own suppressed-tier evidence against top competition. Ceiling: high, contingent on Goff clearing his documented top-tier suppression pattern. Primary risk: if Goff's production suppresses toward his top-tier-defense norm (226.9 pass yards/game), this stack underperforms as a unit, not just individually.

**Secondary Stack — New Orleans Double/Triple: Tyler Shough + Chris Olave (+ Juwan Johnson)**
Correlation logic: Olave is Shough's clear focal point (29.4% target share) and Johnson is the second-most-targeted player in the entire New Orleans passing game — this stack captures the two most stable volume pieces in an otherwise thin receiving corps. Matchup support is real and specific: Olave's likely path runs through Amik Robertson, Detroit's most exploitable coverage piece (Finding 3). Salary: Shough ($5,600) + Olave ($6,700) = $12,300 (24.6% of cap); adding Johnson ($3,700) brings the triple to $16,000 (32% of cap) — notably cheaper than the Detroit primary stack for comparable target-share coverage. Per Finding 1, **do not add Kamara to this stack** — his best games arrive when this exact stack underperforms, not alongside it.

## 8. DFS RISK ASSESSMENT

The single largest lineup risk in this game is **Jahmyr Gibbs' Questionable tag**, compounded by the fact that his most logical direct beneficiary, David Montgomery, does not appear on this DK slate at all — there is no clean, evidence-backed pivot available on this slate if Gibbs sits, which raises the stakes of confirming his status before locking any Gibbs-anchored build.

Secondary risks: **Sam LaPorta's incomplete 2025 log** (evidence runs only through Week 10) creates real availability uncertainty the DK slate's lack of an injury tag does not resolve. **New Orleans' quarterback situation** carries structural instability beyond this single game — Shough and Rattler split starts materially in 2025, and nothing in the evidence confirms Shough's job is fully secure. **Kamara's workload** is genuinely split with Devin Neal and, more recently, Audric Estimé, whose two-game trend is real but thin. Finally, **stack-construction risk** is elevated on the New Orleans side specifically — combining Shough's passing stack with Kamara fights the offense's own documented tendencies (Finding 1), a risk unique to lineup-building rather than to any individual player's evidence.

## 9. DFS TAKEAWAY

If this matchup teaches one lesson, it's this: New Orleans' two best offensive weapons are not a "pick one" question the way most backfield-versus-passing-game decisions usually are — Shough's passing game and Kamara's rushing game move in opposite directions from each other on a game-by-game basis, so a lineup betting on one is implicitly betting against the other. Build the Shough/Olave/Johnson passing correlation and pair it with Estimé or Devin Neal, not Kamara. On the other side of the ball, the opposite logic applies to Detroit — Goff and Gibbs are a genuinely additive pair, and the better lineup construction question there isn't which one to fade, but whether the combined salary of rostering both leaves enough room to also capture Olave's price-relative-to-St.-Brown leverage on the other side of the field.
